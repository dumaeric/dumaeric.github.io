TOPIC 02

Surveillance Capitalism

A Hypothetical Scenario

You’re at the restaurant with your friends, talking about your cute but fat cat. The consensus is that something needs to be done. Your real-life Garfield needs to be put on a diet. You are back at your place, Garfield meows for food (again), and you open your phone. What a coincidence! Instagram shows you an advertisement for diet cat food.

But what if this is not a coincidence at all? What if your phone could listen to your conversations and monetize that information? What if every online and offline activity can be translated into data so somebody or something can get better at selling you something? This is not a hypothetical situation. This is the era of surveillance capitalism.

What Is Surveillance Capitalism?

Surveillance capitalism is a term popularized by Shoshana Zuboff’s book, The Age of Surveillance Capitalism: The Fight for a Human Future at the New Frontier of Power. It describes a market where consumer data is bought and sold as a commodity, and the data is obtained through large-scale surveillance.

This panopticon started with the likes of Google, Amazon, Apple, and Facebook, who realized they could earn more money by collecting customer data to provide targeted ads. Thanks to them, you could watch “The Notebook” and, the second you finish the movie, receive an ad for a special offer at your favorite fast food restaurant. Thank you, oligopolies! You are very comforting.

The business practice spread like a disease when other economic agents became aware of its profitability. The progenitors of surveillance capitalism broadened their horizons by selling the data to third-party platforms.

Similarly, surveillance capitalism created its strongest soldiers and named them data brokers. Data brokers obtain data from various sources, ranging from your online searches to financial transactions made at your local grocery store. An exhaustive consumer profile is created, containing data such as age, sex, height, weight, education, and lifestyle choices. Based on the profile, an individual is assigned to the different categories he or she pertains to. Some might get “sports enthusiast,” others “single mom,” or even “diabetes patient.” Your identity can then be sold to companies interested in selling you something based on it.

Real Life Examples

A report from the University of Cambridge’s Minderoo Centre for Technology and Democracy and a BBC article show that even women’s menstrual cycles have been commodified. Menstrual data is considered healthcare data in the UK and the EU, whereas the US considers it wellness data.

The report underscores that media, non-profit, and consumer groups’ investigations have revealed that the apps have shared data with third parties like advertisers, data brokers, and tech giants. Having such personal data disclosed could harm career prospects or make obtaining insurance harder and more expensive. In the United Kingdom, menstrual cycle apps have been used to convict women of performing illegal abortions.

In a different example, an Australian medical appointment app, HealthEngine, was found to be selling patient data to law firms. The law firms would then call people who made appointments for workplace injuries, informing them about the possibility of suing and receiving compensation. HealthEngine did not commit any illegality because operations such as giving data to third-party organizations are part of the terms and conditions that users must agree to in order to use the app. Moreover, Australian legislation is not comprehensive enough to prevent data sharing.

Does a Solution Exist?

Company CEOs would say that those who have nothing to hide should not be afraid of surveillance capitalism. Shoshana Zuboff rebukes these claims, stating, “If you have nothing to hide then you are nothing.”

The examples of menstrual cycle apps and health appointment apps showcase that there is a gap in legislation that needs to be filled. Solving this might prevent third-party access to data in some situations, but not overall reduce it. Companies love to slither their way through legal loopholes.

Nevertheless, citizens should ask themselves whether it is in their government’s interest to regulate this nefarious business model. After all, we saw in the state surveillance example that intelligence services also rely on data obtained from social media apps. Fighting the surveillance capitalism “Goliath” may harm the collaboration between intelligence services and platforms.
