TOPIC 01

State Surveillance

Why do states surveil?

It is not shocking that every government engages in some form of surveillance. Domestically, states gaze at their subjects to pacify and manage them, collecting data to ensure everything from welfare provision to a smooth electoral process.

There are multiple reasons as to why states conduct mass surveillance, one of them being the fight against terrorism. However, researchers suggest that all those justifications can be tied to the argument of ensuring national security. According to them, “the work of intelligence is said to begin from suspicion of dangerous acts committed by a group under surveillance.” Consequently, surveillance in the name of national security often takes preventive, pre-emptive, and predictive forms that can be carried out before any risk is identified.

Policy and Procurement

The surveillance policy process is opaque, to say the least. It is hidden away in the corridors of the ministries of defence and homeland security, which tend to represent their own bubble world within the state. In most cases, there is no policy process at all.

In the US, local security agencies apply to federal programs to receive surveillance technology. The legislative and civil society are not involved whatsoever in the procurement process, which prevents effective regulation of what can and cannot be done with the technology. In her research on surveillance technology procurement, Catherine Crump illustrates how the Seattle Police Department secretly acquired a surveillance drone and a network of public surveillance cameras. The public found out about the drone when an NGO came across the Seattle Police Department on a Federal Aviation Administration-disclosed list of drone users.

What does the state do with the data?

We have already established that governments employ various surveillance methods to collect data. It would be puerile to believe that the data is analysed and then stored somewhere. The UK government uses its data repertoire for various purposes, such as analysing the trust and risk of citizens, predicting and profiling their behaviour, enforcing compliance, protecting the public, and sustaining collaboration between state agencies and private companies. Interestingly, most data can abet both the service state and the surveillance state, suggesting coexistence rather than mutual exclusion.

In their satellite monitoring example, police rely on private companies to monitor offenders on probation. The satellite data is firstly used to ensure safety by monitoring offenders and keeping them away from off-limit regions. However, the state engaged in surveillance when the police force started using the same data for crime detection and prevention. This surveillance behaviour is described as data sharing and function creep, i.e., the state using citizens’ data beyond its intended purpose.

Likewise, for driver licence applications, the applicant’s personal information is cross-checked with existing data from multiple institutional sources. The applicant then receives a score which describes how likely it is that the applicant is who they claim to be. Only applicants with a high enough score can conclude their application. Research describes this example as a form of surveillance because it effectively creates layered citizenship based on the quality and quantity of data the state possesses on its citizens. Only those who are “surveilled well enough" get to receive the service.

Modern State Surveillance

State surveillance has transformed immensely over the years. There are three dimensions to modern state surveillance: transnationalization, digitization, and privatization. Transnationalization and privatization are by far the most riveting.

By the end of WWII, national intelligence agencies had developed considerable ties with each other, which facilitated collaboration mainly through data sharing. One such example is the “Five Eyes,” the network represented by the US, New Zealand, Australia, Canada, and the UK. At the moment, US and European intelligence services work together in networks to gather and share internet data from underwater cables.

This raises a plethora of problems. Firstly, transnationalization, and most importantly, data harvesting from underwater internet cables, justifies surveilling other states in the name of national security. Moreover, transnationalization can challenge and ignore the authority of the “professionals of politics.” Last but not least, data sharing implies that states disregard the data privacy of their citizens. When the state surveils its people, this can be justified by safeguarding national security. However, giving citizens’ data to other intelligence agencies blurs an ethical line that was already blurry in the first place.

The privatization of state surveillance refers to states using private providers for data mining. The collaboration can be voluntary or forceful. According to academics, intelligence agencies work with the likes of Facebook, Instagram, YouTube, Skype, Google, and Apple. They also collaborate with telecommunication companies like Verizon Business, Vodafone Cable, and Global Crossing.
