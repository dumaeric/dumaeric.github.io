Edward Snowden

Who is Snowden?

Imagine yourself as a government agent tasked with monitoring citizens in the name of social security and national interests. One day, you decided to give it all up and tell it to the world, move to the biggest rival of your own country. You are Edward Snowden,  a former American intelligence contractor who worked for the Central Intelligence Agency (CIA) and later for the National Security Agency (NSA) through private contractors. Through his work, he gained access to classified information about government surveillance programs.

In 2013, Snowden provided classified documents to journalists from The Guardian and The Washington Post. He left the United States and travelled to Hong Kong, where he met journalists and disclosed information about the programs. He later arrived in Russia, where he remained after the United States revoked his passport while he was travelling. He is now a naturalized Russian citizen. 

His actions made him one of the most controversial figures in modern intelligence history. Supporters regard him as a whistleblower who exposed government overreach, while critics argue that his disclosures endangered national security by revealing classified intelligence capabilities. 

What did he do?

Snowden's central action was to disclose classified documents demonstrating the scale and methods of American electronic surveillance.

One of the most significant revelations concerned the NSA's collection of telephone metadata from millions of Americans. This information included details such as the numbers involved in calls, their dates, times, and duration, rather than necessarily the conversations themselves.

Another major disclosure concerned PRISM, a surveillance program through which the NSA obtained communications and other information from major internet service providers under a legal framework. The precise scope, legal basis, and operation of individual surveillance programs differed, but the documents demonstrated how intelligence agencies could obtain and analyse substantial amounts of digital information.

What were the contents?

1. Mass collection of communications data

The NSA's telephone records program demonstrated that intelligence agencies could collect metadata on a very large scale. Although metadata does not necessarily contain the content of a conversation, it can reveal patterns of communication, social relationships, and personal routines when analysed over time.

2. Internet surveillance and PRISM

The PRISM disclosures showed how intelligence agencies could acquire communications and other digital information from major online services under applicable legal authorities. The revelations raised questions about the relationship between technology companies, intelligence agencies, and the legal protections afforded to users.

3. International surveillance

The disclosures also showed that surveillance was not confined to American citizens or American territory. Intelligence agencies monitored foreign communications and conducted intelligence operations involving international leaders, diplomats, and institutions. These revelations strained diplomatic relationships and raised concerns about national sovereignty.

4. The reach of digital monitoring

Phone calls, internet communications, and other digital records could now potentially become part of intelligence analysis, depending on the program, legal authority, and target. Together, these revelations showed that the digital services people use every day had become part of a much larger environment in which governments could collect, retain, and analyse information.

The Work of Intelligence Services

The documents revealed that intelligence agencies had developed extensive systems for collecting and analysing communications data, sometimes affecting people who were not suspected of criminal activity. The US government justified many of these activities as necessary to identify terrorist threats and protect national security. A record of who communicates with whom, and when, can reveal relationships and behavioural patterns even without access to the actual conversation. When combined with other information, such records can expose aspects of a person's private life.

However, the revelations showed that the public had limited visibility into the scale of these operations and the legal interpretations supporting them. It also raised questions about the unequal relationship between governments and citizens. Intelligence agencies possessed sophisticated technical capabilities, while ordinary people often lacked the information necessary to understand how their data was collected or how to challenge its use.

The Aftermath

1. Legal reforms

In the United States, the disclosures helped generate pressure for reform of intelligence surveillance. The USA FREEDOM Act of 2015 ended the NSA's previous bulk telephone-record collection program under Section 215 of the Patriot Act and established a different framework for accessing certain records.

Courts also examined the legality of surveillance practices. In 2015, a federal appeals court concluded that the bulk telephone-record collection program exceeded the authority granted by Section 215. 

However, the reforms did not eliminate all forms of mass surveillance, and debates over intelligence powers continued.

2. Greater attention to digital privacy

Technology companies faced increasing public pressure to protect user information. Major companies strengthened aspects of their security practices, including encryption, while privacy became a more prominent consideration in discussions about digital services.

Encryption helps protect communications from unauthorized access, although its effectiveness depends on how a particular service is designed and implemented. Snowden's disclosures helped make these technical questions part of mainstream public debate.

3. International diplomatic tensions

The revelations damaged trust between the United States and some of its allies, particularly after reports that American intelligence agencies had monitored foreign leaders and diplomatic communications.

This raised questions about whether intelligence cooperation could coexist with respect for national sovereignty and the privacy of their citizens. The disclosures also encouraged other countries to reconsider their own surveillance practices and digital-security policies.

4. A stronger public debate about human rights

Organisations such as Amnesty International argued that mass surveillance threatened the right to privacy and called for stronger legal safeguards. The United Nations and other international institutions also gave greater attention to privacy in the digital age.

Snowden's case became a reference point about whistleblowing, freedom of expression, government secrecy, and the responsibilities of people working inside powerful institutions.

5. The unknown future

Despite these changes, the long-term effects remain contested. As The Guardian observed in its 2023 retrospective, the disclosures substantially improved public understanding of government surveillance, but their impact on the overall protection of privacy was less conclusive. Some reforms were enacted, while other surveillance authorities remained in place. New technologies also created additional challenges for governments, businesses, and citizens.

The aftermath therefore cannot be measured only by the laws that changed but also by whether governments have become more transparent, whether oversight has become more effective, and whether individuals have gained meaningful control over their personal information.
